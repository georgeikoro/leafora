What a plant actually does to a room

The air-purifying claim is mostly wrong, and the real effect is more interesting than the one on the label.

12 February 2026

The claim is a familiar one: one plant per hundred square feet, and the air is clean. It comes from a NASA study in 1989, it is repeated on plant labels to this day, and it does not survive contact with a real room.

What the study actually did

The experiment sealed plants in chambers roughly the size of a wardrobe, pumped in a single pollutant, and measured what came out. In that setting, leaves and root bacteria did remove volatile compounds. The chamber, though, is the entire trick. It has no doors, no windows, no draughts, and no forty cubic metres of air being changed twice an hour by the ordinary leakiness of a building.

When later researchers scaled the numbers up to a real room, the figure that came back was somewhere between ten and a thousand plants to match what one open window does in an hour. The honest version is this: a monstera is not an air filter, and anyone selling you one on that basis is guessing that you will not check.

A plant will not clean your air. It will change how you behave in the room, and that turns out to matter more.

The effect that does hold up

Attention restoration is the more durable finding, and it is less glamorous. Looking at something living, at a middle distance, for a few seconds at a time, lets the part of your attention that has been gripping something else let go. It is the same reason a window onto a tree is worth more than a window onto a wall, and it shows up in recovery times, in error rates, in reported stress. The plant is not medicating you. It is giving your eyes somewhere undemanding to rest.

There is a second effect that nobody measures because it is too obvious: keeping a plant alive requires you to notice it. Once a week you look closely at something that is not a screen, decide whether it needs water, and act. For a lot of people that is the only unhurried, unprompted attention they pay to anything all week.

A note on humidity

Plants do transpire, and a group of them in a small room will lift local humidity by a few per cent. In a dry flat in February that is pleasant. It is not a humidifier, and it will not solve a genuine damp-air problem in either direction. Group them if you like the effect. Do not buy them for it.

The real reason to buy one

A room with something growing in it reads differently, and the fortnightly ritual of checking on it is good for you in a way that has nothing to do with particulates. That is a smaller claim than the one on the label, but it holds up.
